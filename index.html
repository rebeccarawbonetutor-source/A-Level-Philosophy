Epistemology Unit 4: The limits of knowledge

The limits of knowledge
Scepticism and Descartes' method

Philosophical scepticism

Sceptical foundations

Nature of philosophical scepticism vs normal incredulity

  • Normal incredulity: Doubt that arises about a specific everyday claim based on other background beliefs you do not doubt, where the reasons for this doubt can, in principle, be removed or investigated through standard empirical inquiry. Examples include doubting whether a friend's birthday is August 17th or 18th, or questioning whether a local shop is open.
  • Philosophical scepticism: The view that we lack knowledge in such a way that the reasons for doubt arguably cannot be removed. It claims that almost none of our usual forms of justification are adequate and challenges the fundamental security of human belief systems.
  • Local scepticism: Scepticism restricted to a specific domain of knowledge or type of justification, such as doubting claims about the past, future induction, or the contents of other minds (e.g., wondering if a smiling friend is truly happy inside).
  • Global scepticism: Radically sweeping scepticism applied to all knowledge claims simultaneously, asserting that we have no knowledge and that none of our usual cognitive justifications are adequate. This is vividly illustrated by thought experiments like the brain in a vat scenario.
  • The infinite regress argument: Challenges the foundational structure of justification by showing that any belief requires support from another belief, leading either to an infinite chain of reasons, circular reasoning, or arbitrary dogmatism.
  • Foundationalist responses: Most epistemologists respond to the regress problem via foundationalism, arguing that the regress terminates in self-justified basic beliefs—such as perceptual, memory, logical, or introspective beliefs—which do not require further support and serve to justify all other derived beliefs.
  • Role and function of scepticism: Philosophers engage in sceptical worry not because they genuinely doubt their everyday lives or memory, but to rigorously test, analyse, and clarify the deep conceptual frameworks underlying human thought.

Descartes' method of doubt

Rationalist method

The three waves of doubt and foundational restructuring

  • Infallibilism and the demolition project: Descartes operates as an infallibilist who demands absolute certainty, resolving to use the bulldozer of doubt to sweep away all previous uncertain opinions and rebuild his entire belief system from scratch on unbreakable foundations.
  • Wave 1 (Illusion): Challenges sensory reliability because the senses have occasionally deceived us regarding small, distant, or poorly lit objects. However, Descartes notes this does not give reason to doubt all sensory experience, as near and clear perceptions (like sitting by the fire in a dressing gown) seem completely certain.
  • Wave 2 (Dreaming): Poses the hypothesis that we might think we are awake when we are actually dreaming, as dreams can vividly mimic waking reality. While this undermines immediate sensory claims about the physical world, Descartes argues that basic formal concepts common to both dreams and reality—such as space, general magnitude, time, and basic mathematical truths—remain seemingly safe.
  • Wave 3 (The evil demon): The ultimate radical hypothesis that an all-powerful, malicious demon is expending all his energy to systematically deceive us about everything, including arithmetic, geometry, and basic logical structures.
  • Criticisms of total doubt: David Hume argued in the Enquiry that if one doubted one's own faculties entirely, no reasoning could ever bring us back to conviction, making total Cartesian doubt self-defeating and entirely incurable.
Responses to scepticism

Descartes' response

Rationalist recovery

The cogito as an a priori intuition

  • The cogito: Even under the control of an all-powerful evil demon, the act of doubting requires a thinking subject; thus "I think, therefore I am" is grasped directly as an indubitable a priori intuition that survives all sceptical scenarios.

Deductive argument for the existence of God

  • Deductive formulation:
    • Premise 1: I have an idea of God as an infinite, supreme, and perfect being.
    • Premise 2: There must be at least as much reality in the cause as in the effect (the causal principle).
    • Premise 3: As a finite and imperfect being, I could not have caused this idea of infinite perfection myself.
    • Conclusion: Therefore, God must exist as the external cause of my idea.

Deductive argument for the existence of the external world

  • Deductive formulation:
    • Premise 1: I experience passive, vivid, and involuntary sensory ideas that occur against my will.
    • Premise 2: These ideas must have an active cause distinct from myself, as my mind is unextended and cannot produce ideas of extension.
    • Premise 3: God exists, is supremely perfect, and is not a deceiver; He would not allow me to be deceived into thinking physical objects cause my perceptions if they did not exist.
    • Conclusion: Therefore, the external physical world of material bodies exists.

Empiricist responses

Empiricist defences

Locke, Berkeley, Russell and Hume

  • John Locke: Argues against scepticism by highlighting the involuntary nature of sense experience (we cannot choose what sensory inputs we receive) and the robust coherence across different sensory modalities.
  • George Berkeley: Rejects material substance to solve sceptical dilemmas, arguing instead that ordinary objects are ideas in God's mind, and because God causes our perceptions directly and consistently, they constitute genuine reality.
  • Bertrand Russell: Rejects infallibilism and argues that the existence of an independent external world is the best hypothesis to explain why our experiences exhibit regular patterns, coherence, and continuity, such as apples continuing to exist in drawers when unobserved.
  • David Hume's mitigated scepticism: Argues that excessive philosophical scepticism cannot be maintained in active life because human action, employment, and the occupations of common life cause radical doubts to vanish like smoke, leaving us on par with non-philosophers.

Reliabilism

Externalist solution

Sidestepping scepticism through reliable cognitive processes

  • Definition: Knowledge is defined as a True Belief formed by a reliable cognitive process, where reliability means a high ratio of true to false beliefs produced in the actual world.
  • Externalist approach: Reliabilism is externalist because you do not need internal reflective access or logical proof to rule out radical sceptical scenarios, like being a brain in a vat, in order to have knowledge.
  • Scenario analysis: In Scenario 1, the real world, perception is reliable, leading to a true belief, so you know you have hands. In Scenario 2, the brain in a vat, the process is unreliable, so you lack knowledge.
  • Practical resolution: We do not need to know which scenario we are in to possess knowledge; if we inhabit the actual world, our reliable processes grant us knowledge despite our inability to prove from the inside that we are not deceived.
  • Evaluation: Solves scepticism practically by changing the rules and rejecting internalist justification demands, but concedes that in a demon-world, knowledge would be absent because belief-forming processes would fail to be truth-conducive.
