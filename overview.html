Overview of dualism
Core dualist theories

Substance dualism

Unit 1

Minds exist and are not identical to bodies or to parts of bodies.

The indivisibility argument for substance dualism (Descartes)

  1. The body is spatially extended and divisible.
  2. The mind is unextended and indivisible.
  3. By Leibniz's law, if entities have different properties, they are distinct.
  4. Therefore, the mind is not identical to the body.

Responses, including:

  • The mental is divisible in some sense: Psychological studies and split-brain cases indicate division within mental phenomena.
  • Not everything thought of as physical is divisible: Fundamental physical properties or forces may not be divisible parts.

The conceivability argument for substance dualism (expressed without reference to God) (Descartes)

  1. If I can clearly and distinctly conceive of the mind existing without the body, it is metaphysically possible.
  2. I can clearly and distinctly conceive of my mind existing without my physical body.
  3. Therefore, it is metaphysically possible for the mind to exist without the body.
  4. If the mind and body can exist independently, they are distinct substances.

Responses including:

  • Mind without body is not conceivable: Logical behaviourism or verificationist frameworks reject disembodied mind concepts as incoherent.
  • What is conceivable may not be metaphysically possible: Conceivability fails to guarantee metaphysical possibility due to hidden essential natures (such as water necessarily being H2O).
  • What is metaphysically possible tells us nothing about the actual world: Possibility in the abstract does not entail actual physical structures in our universe.

Property dualism

Unit 3

There are at least some mental properties that are neither reducible to nor supervenient upon physical properties.

The ‘philosophical zombies’ argument for property dualism (David Chalmers)

  1. It is conceivable that there could be a being physically identical to a human but lacking consciousness (a zombie world).
  2. If something is conceivable without contradiction, then it is metaphysically possible.
  3. A zombie world is conceivable—a universe physically identical to ours but without consciousness.
  4. If a metaphysically possible world can be physically identical to ours yet lack consciousness, physical facts alone do not entail consciousness.
  5. Therefore, consciousness must be a non-physical component of reality.

Responses including:

  • A 'philosophical zombie' / a 'zombie' world is not conceivable: Dennett argues that removing consciousness while keeping function intact is conceptually incoherent.
  • What is conceivable may not be metaphysically possible: Conceivability is constrained by hidden metaphysical necessities.
  • What is metaphysically possible tells us nothing about the actual world: Papineau argues abstract possibility does not refute actual physicalism.

The ‘knowledge/Mary’ argument for property dualism (Frank Jackson)

  1. Mary has complete physical information about colour vision while confined to her black-and-white environment.
  2. When released, Mary learns something new about the phenomenal properties of colour vision.
  3. Therefore, her previous physical knowledge was incomplete.
  4. Therefore, reductive physicalism is false.

Responses including:

  • Mary does not gain new propositional knowledge but does gain ability knowledge: Lewis argues she acquires "know-how" rather than new facts.
  • Mary does not gain new propositional knowledge but does gain acquaintance knowledge: She gains direct familiarity with redness rather than non-physical facts.
  • Mary gains new propositional knowledge, but this is knowledge of physical facts that she already knew in a different way: Papineau's old fact / new phenomenal concept response.
Issues facing dualism

Issues facing dualism, including:

Unit 2

The problem of other minds

  • Core challenge: First-person priority makes inferring hidden internal mental states from third-person physical behavior problematic[cite: 1].

Responses including:

  • The argument from analogy: Inferring others have minds because their behavior resembles our own[cite: 1].
  • The existence of other minds is the best hypothesis: Abductive reasoning where mindedness best explains physical behavior[cite: 1].

Dualism makes a "category mistake" (Gilbert Ryle)

  • Core criticism: Treating mental states as non-physical occult objects ("ghost in the machine") miscategorises dispositional concepts into wrong logical types[cite: 1].

Issues facing interactionist dualism, including:

Interaction

The conceptual interaction problem (as articulated by Elisabeth, Princess of Bohemia)

  • Core challenge: Causation requires contact and extension through physical impulsion, which an unextended mind lacks[cite: 1].

The empirical interaction problem

  • Core challenge: Non-physical mental inputs causing physical brain changes would violate conservation of mass-energy laws[cite: 1].
Issues facing epiphenomenalism

Issues facing epiphenomenalist dualism, including:

Epiphenomenalism

Challenges arising from the causal impotence of mental properties[cite: 1].

The challenge posed by introspective self-knowledge

  • Core challenge: If mental states are causally impotent, phenomenal feelings cannot cause thoughts or speech about them, making genuine self-knowledge impossible.

The challenge posed by the phenomenology of our mental life (ie as involving causal connections, both psychological and psycho-physical)

  • Core challenge: Lived experience presents continuous causal interactions (physical-to-mental and mental-to-physical), which epiphenomenalism reduces to an illusion.

The challenge posed by natural selection/evolution

  • Core challenge: Natural selection only tracks survival traits; epiphenomenal traits with zero physical survival impact cannot be selected for by evolution.
